The hourglass arrangement changes the design fundamentally. Instead of trying to conduct heat through a large, stationary mass of sand to one radiating end, you would use gravity to transport the hot material itself from an insulated storage vessel into a separate heat-delivery vessel.

That is a promising way to address the poor heat conduction of stationary sand. It also gives you a natural control variable: the rate at which sand flows determines how quickly thermal energy is transferred.

I would treat it as a two-vessel, gravity-fed thermal battery:

* Upper vessel: charges the sand to around 800°C and retains it during the 8-hour waiting period.

* Narrow transfer neck: meters the flow during discharge and opens fully for rapid return.

* Lower vessel: receives the hot sand and transfers its heat to the room through a designed heat exchanger or radiating surface.

* Rotation mechanism: switches the relative positions of the vessels so gravity can reverse the transfer direction.

There is one important distinction: pouring hot sand into the lower vessel does not itself guarantee 300 W of useful heat for 10 hours. The lower vessel must extract that heat at the right rate, and the upper vessel must retain enough energy during the 8-hour hold.

### 1\. The energy and sand-flow requirements

The important advantage is that moving the sand lets you bypass the long conductive path through a stationary sand mass. A controlled moving bed is an established thermal-storage concept; the challenge is to get predictable flow and good heat transfer from the grains to the receiver.

For the calculations below, I use the same approximate heat-capacity assumptions as before: from 20–300°C and from 300–800°C. These are approximations; published measurements of high-purity silica sand show temperature-dependent heat capacity and a quartz phase transition near 573°C.

The charging-power figures are theoretical minima for heating the sand from 20°C to 800°C, before heating the metalwork or losing energy through the insulation.

Your original 500 W heater supplies only 2.5 kWh in five hours. It cannot supply even the 3 kWh required for 300 W over ten hours, let alone the 5.4 kWh required for eighteen hours.

The revised architecture therefore needs more charging power, effective thermal insulation and enough sand to cover the losses.

### 2\. The first mechanical correction: which way does the hourglass face?

For a conventional two-chamber hourglass, the simplest cycle is:

Phase A — Charge and hold

Chamber A is above chamber B. Fill A with the sand, close the gate and charge A to 800°C. Leave A above B throughout the eight-hour retention period.

Phase B — Meter the hot sand downward

Open the gate and transfer the sand from A to B over ten hours. B acts as the heat-delivery vessel, with a designed heat exchanger and exposed radiator.

Phase C — Return the sand

After A has emptied and B has collected the sand, close the gate and invert the assembly so B is uppermost. Open the gate fully and return the sand from B to A quickly. Close the gate and restore A to the upper position for the next charge.

This order matters. Inverting the whole assembly before the filled upper chamber discharges can put the sand away from the transfer neck. A filled chamber must be above its receiving chamber when its outlet opens.

The arrangement also needs to accommodate electrical wiring for the heater, supports and bearings, and a safe way to operate the gate without approaching an 800°C surface. I would treat the rotating assembly as a mechanical machine with an interlocked gate, not as a flask intended to be flipped by hand while hot.

### 3\. The narrow neck is the critical component

A small stainless-steel pipe looks attractive because it is cheap and could serve as both the transfer path and the thermal bridge between chambers. Unfortunately, making the pipe very narrow is not a reliable way to meter hot granular material.

Dry sand can stop flowing because grains bridge across an outlet, fines accumulate, or friction and particle properties change. Bulk-solids engineering explicitly treats outlet geometry and arching as separate design problems; a flow rate cannot reliably be specified from the pipe diameter alone.

Consider the target of roughly 1.6 kg/h. With ordinary fine sand, the opening needed to restrict gravity flow to such a low rate may be only a few millimetres across. That makes the system vulnerable to blockage. Increasing the opening, meanwhile, can make the sand discharge much faster than intended.

I would separate the two functions.

Use a generous, refractory-lined transfer throat and a separate metering gate. The gate should control the amount of material released, while the throat remains large enough to avoid routine bridging. A refractory slide gate or a purpose-built high-temperature granular feeder is a more credible starting point than an ordinary plumbing valve. Fine sand is abrasive, and grains caught in a hot valve can prevent it from closing or reopening.

A second issue is gas displacement. As sand leaves A and enters B, gas must be able to travel back from B to A. Otherwise, a pressure difference can resist the falling sand or interrupt the flow. A separate gas-equalisation path is preferable to forcing gas to move counter-currently through the same narrow throat.

The transfer neck also conducts heat between chambers. Its geometry should limit this thermal bridge during the eight-hour hold while still allowing reliable material transfer. A stainless tube alone does not create thermal isolation.

### 4\. The lower vessel should be a heat exchanger, not a bucket

This is where I would make the largest design change.

If the hot sand simply drops into a large lower cylinder, the grains at the walls may cool while the centre of the accumulated bed remains hot. The lower cylinder then becomes a slowly responding thermal mass, and the heat output depends on the sand-to-wall contact, the growing pile and the radiator temperature—not just on the sand flow rate.

Instead, arrange for the incoming sand to transfer heat through a relatively thin, moving layer that passes close to substantial metal or refractory heat-transfer surfaces. The receiver can then collect the sand after it has passed through the active heat-transfer region.

The external side of that heat exchanger should be designed for the intended room-heating output. A practical arrangement would have an exposed finned surface or a controlled radiator, with the hot sand passing close to the internal heat-transfer surfaces. Moving-bed heat exchangers are studied specifically because the granular flow pattern determines how effectively the material exchanges heat.

The target flow can be calculated from the enthalpy drop only if the sand is actually cooled by a known amount while passing through the heat exchanger. For example, cooling from 800°C to 150°C at a flow rate of 1.61 kg/h corresponds to an ideal thermal transfer rate of approximately 300 W. A collection bucket does not automatically achieve that temperature drop.

There is also a control advantage in separating the heat exchanger from the storage chamber: you can meter the sand transfer while using the exposed radiator or a mechanical shutter to prevent an excessive initial heat output.

### 5\. The eight-hour hold may be more difficult than the sand transfer

I would not rely on an argon-filled annulus as the primary insulation for an 800°C store.

NIST data gives argon thermal conductivity of approximately 30 mW/(m·K) around 600 K, rising at higher temperatures. Its conductivity is lower than air's, but it remains a gas capable of transporting substantial heat.

Radiative exchange is a further concern. Stainless steel oxidised by high-temperature operation can have high emissivity; published emissivity tables report approximately 0.85 for oxidised 18-8 stainless steel. A large empty gap between hot, oxidised steel surfaces is therefore not equivalent to a well-designed vacuum flask.

For this concept, I would investigate substantial high-temperature refractory insulation around the storage chamber, protected inside a metal casing. If vacuum construction is excluded, the design must accept either a larger insulation thickness, more standby loss, a lower storage temperature, or some combination of those trade-offs. The losses need to be measured before the final sand mass and charging power can be selected.

The rotating transfer neck must not undermine that insulation. A continuous metal connection between the hot chamber and its surroundings can carry heat out even when the rest of the vessel is well insulated.

### 6\. What I would prototype first

I would begin with a smaller, non-rotating test rig at a substantially lower temperature to validate the granular transfer and heat-exchanger behaviour before attempting an 800°C rotating machine. The main measurements would be mass flow, sand temperature before and after the receiver, radiator temperature, useful heat output and standby temperature loss.

The full design would then be sized around measured performance:

* If the requirement is 300 W for ten hours after the eight-hour hold, the ideal minimum is 3 kWh delivered and about 16 kg of sand cooled from 800°C to 150°C. The practical mass must be higher to cover storage and transfer losses.

* If the original requirement of 300 W for eighteen hours of actual heat delivery still applies, the ideal minimum rises to 5.4 kWh and about 29 kg of sand cooled to 150°C. The discharge period itself must support eighteen hours, not just the combined eight-hour hold and ten-hour transfer period.

The most promising version is therefore an insulated hot-storage chamber, a deliberately engineered high-temperature granular gate, and a separate lower heat exchanger. The hourglass principle can reduce reliance on conduction through stationary sand, but the design will succeed or fail on dependable material flow, standby insulation and heat extraction—not simply on reaching 800°C.
