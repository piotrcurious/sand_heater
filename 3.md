Worked for 4m 18s

The third shell and a substantial mineral-wool jacket can make the storage stage much more plausible. With 40 kg of sand, however, the design looks reasonably promising for 300 W over the 10-hour discharge period, but marginal for 300 W over 18 hours after an 8-hour hold. The decisive variables are insulation thickness, the hot-to-cold flange, and how much heat the lower cylinder can actually transfer to the room.

Here is the rough calculation using inexpensive construction methods, without assuming a better vacuum or exotic materials.

### 1\. The 40 kg sand budget

High-purity silica sand has a bulk density around 1,543 kg/m³ and an average heat capacity above 1.1 kJ/(kg·K) over 300–1,200°C, according to an NREL thermal-storage study. Its heat capacity varies with temperature.

Using the same approximate assumptions as before:

| Quantity                                  | Estimate    |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------- |
| Sand mass                                 | 40 kg       |
| Packed sand volume                        | 25.9 litres |
| Energy to heat sand from 20°C to 800°C    | 8.6 kWh     |
| Heat released cooling from 800°C to 150°C | 7.44 kWh    |
| Target: 300 W for 10 hours                | 3.0 kWh     |
| Target: 300 W for 18 hours                | 5.4 kWh     |

The 7.44 kWh figure is the ideal heat available from the sand alone, assuming it really reaches 800°C and can cool to 150°C while transferring its heat usefully.

That gives 2.04 kWh of headroom above the 18-hour target. The metalwork adds some heat capacity, but standby losses, temperature gradients and heat-transfer limitations consume that margin.

For a 40 kg store, a 400 mm internal diameter cylinder filled to approximately 210 mm holds the required sand volume. Allow extra height for the heater, headspace and internal supports; approximately 280 mm of internal height is a reasonable geometric starting point.

### 2\. Add the third shell and 150–250 mm of insulation

The new insulation should do most of the work. The third steel cylinder is useful as an additional radiation shield, but it cannot compensate for a thin insulation layer or a large metal connection bridging the hot and cold shells.

For a first-order model, assume an effective insulated surface area of 0.6 m² and an effective mineral-wool conductivity of 0.08 W/(m·K). The latter is an illustrative high-temperature value; actual conductivity depends on the product and its mean operating temperature. Industrial mineral-wool data show that conductivity can rise significantly as the mean temperature increases.

The simplified initial heat-leak estimate is

\\dot Q \\approx \\frac{kA\\Delta T}{d},

where is insulation thickness and K.

### Estimated standby insulation losses

One-layer approximation for a 780 K temperature difference. The actual three-shell assembly will differ.

| Wool thickness | Initial heat leak | Approx. 8 h energy loss |
| -------------- | ----------------- | ----------------------- |
| 100 mm         | 374 W             | 2.6 kWh                 |
| 150 mm         | 250 W             | 1.8 kWh                 |
| 200 mm         | 187 W             | 1.4 kWh                 |
| 250 mm         | 150 W             | 1.1 kWh                 |

The eight-hour estimates use a simple lumped thermal-capacity model, including an illustrative 10 kg of hot steel. They are not measured performance figures or guaranteed upper bounds.

These figures assume the entire temperature difference occurs across the wool. Radiation shields and gas gaps can reduce the actual loss; thermal bridges can increase it.

The important result is that 200–250 mm is a more defensible starting range than 50–100 mm for the storage chamber. At these thicknesses, the outside dimensions grow considerably: a 400 mm inner cylinder with 200 mm of radial insulation alone becomes roughly 800 mm across, before allowing for the inner and intermediate shells.

Use industrial insulation selected for the actual hot-face temperature. Ordinary building mineral wool should not be assumed suitable for continuous contact with an 800°C surface. With three shells, the wool should be on the cooler outer side, but you must measure that shell temperature before choosing the product.

### How much energy remains after the hold?

Under the simplified model, the average store temperature after eight hours could be approximately:

| Wool thickness | Illustrative core temperature after hold | Remaining sand heat above 150°C |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| 100 mm         | 600°C                                    | 5.0 kWh                         |
| 150 mm         | 660°C                                    | 5.7 kWh                         |
| 200 mm         | 690°C                                    | 6.1 kWh                         |
| 250 mm         | 710°C                                    | 6.4 kWh                         |

The last column is still ideal heat available from the sand; it is not guaranteed room heat.

This is why the distinction between the two targets matters:

* 300 W for 10 hours after the hold: 3.0 kWh is needed. The 40 kg charge has a reasonably comfortable energy margin with 150–200 mm of insulation.

* 300 W for 18 hours after the hold: 5.4 kWh is needed. With 200 mm insulation, only about 0.7 kWh remains beyond the ideal requirement. That is a narrow margin for a crude moving-bed exchanger. With 250 mm, the margin improves to around 1.0 kWh, but it is still not generous.

There is also a timing issue. An eight-hour period deliberately intended to retain heat is not itself an 8-hour period guaranteed to produce at least 300 W. If your 18-hour target includes the hold, its heat leakage must count as useful room heating and must actually remain above the output cutoff. If the 18 hours begin after the hold, you need 18 hours of discharge after those eight hours, not just ten.

### 3\. The continuous stainless-steel flange is still a major weakness

Adding the third shell does not solve the problem if a broad continuous flange directly joins the hot and cold walls.

For illustration, consider a 400 mm diameter connection with a 1 mm effective conducting thickness and a 20 mm radial conduction path. With stainless steel conductivity of 15 W/(m·K), its simplified conductance is

G \\approx \\frac{k\\pi Dt}{L}
\\approx 0.94\\ \\mathrm{W/K}.

At a 700 K temperature difference, this would imply around 660 W of conduction. A real flange's temperature distribution and geometry will differ, but the order of magnitude shows why the bridge matters.

The cheapest effective improvement is to preserve a long, thin conduction path and minimise the cross-section of any metal connecting the hot vessel to the cooler shells. The third shell should act as a radiation shield rather than being connected to the inner vessel by a broad conducting ring.

### 4\. How well would two steel propane cylinders work as the receiver?

As a geometric example, take two approximately 300 mm diameter cylinders joined end-to-end, with about 1.2 m total height. Their exposed area is roughly 1.2 m².

Assume an oxidised surface emissivity of 0.8, room temperature of 20°C, and natural convection coefficient of 5 W/(m²·K). The approximate combined radiation and convection output would be:

| Outer steel surface temperature | Estimated heat output |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| 40°C                            | 240 W                 |
| 50°C                            | 370 W                 |
| 60°C                            | 510 W                 |
| 80°C                            | 805 W                 |
| 100°C                           | 1.13 kW               |

These are illustrative steady-state surface calculations, not predictions of the sand or shell temperature. Real output varies with the surface finish, airflow, the temperature of the sand and the resistance between the sand and the shell.

The useful conclusion is that a receiver of this size has plenty of emitting area for 300 W. It might have too much exposed area during the early, hotter part of discharge. At a surface temperature of only 50°C, the model already predicts about 370 W.

If the receiver becomes substantially hotter, it could transfer several times the target output. The energy supply limits the sustained average, but it does not guarantee a safe surface temperature: the stored sand and steel can produce a much higher initial output than 300 W.

A simple adjustable insulating jacket or partial shield around the receiver could limit its exposed area. That is more practical than trying to make the narrow sand neck precisely regulate every watt.

### The important qualification about recycled LPG cylinders

I would not use two welded recycled propane bottles as the 800°C receiver. They are made as gas-pressure cylinders, not as furnace vessels intended for sustained high-temperature cycling.

Conventional structural steel loses much of its strength at these temperatures; Eurocode-based data gives a yield-strength reduction factor around 0.11 at 800°C. Steel oxidation, distortion and fatigue also become important when repeatedly cycling a welded shell between room temperature and several hundred degrees.

There is a separate fabrication hazard: an apparently empty LPG cylinder can retain flammable residues. The UK Health and Safety Executive warns that hot work on containers with flammable residues can cause fatal explosions, and requires thorough cleaning and verification before applying heat.

Use the recycled-cylinder dimensions as a size reference, not as an automatic material choice for the hot receiver. A suitable unpressurised steel vessel with known construction and high-temperature suitability is a better basis for the design.

### 5\. The solenoid and nail-style gate

The on/off idea is plausible as a way to control average flow, but restarting the flow should not be treated as a reliable anti-blockage mechanism.

A few practical points:

* The transfer throat needs to be large enough to avoid persistent grain bridging. A very narrow opening can stop flowing even when the valve is fully open.

* A normal fluid solenoid valve is not a suitable hot-sand valve. Keep the electrical actuator outside the hot zone, operating a mechanical gate or pin through a suitably designed stem and high-temperature isolation.

* On/off pulsing can regulate average mass flow, but the actual mass delivered per opening depends on the sand grain size, packing, hopper geometry and the height of the sand column. It needs to be calibrated experimentally.

* The valve and its guides must tolerate abrasive sand, thermal expansion and particles trapped on the sealing surface.

For the planned 10-hour transfer, 40 kg corresponds to an average flow of just 4 kg/h. Sand moving from 800°C down to 150°C carries an ideal enthalpy drop of approximately 0.186 kWh/kg, giving about 0.74 kW of thermal energy flow at 4 kg/h. The target of 300 W therefore requires transferring approximately 40% of that available energy to the room during the ten-hour period. That is an achievable energy balance in principle, but not a guaranteed result from simply pouring sand into a bucket-shaped receiver.

### 6\. The simplest design I would test

Storage chamber A

Approximately 40 kg of dry sand, around 26 litres of packed volume, with a distributed high-temperature heater. Use the third cylinder as a radiation shield and investigate 200–250 mm of appropriate industrial insulation around the storage chamber.

Transfer neck

Keep the throat generous enough for reliable gravity flow. Use a remotely operated mechanical gate, with the actuator located away from the hot zone. Minimise the metal bridge between the insulated store and the receiver.

Receiver B

Use a suitable high-temperature-rated vessel with enough exposed surface to release 300 W. A receiver approximately the size of two domestic gas cylinders has ample area, but a controlled or partially shielded surface is needed to prevent excessive initial output.

The cycle can remain simple: charge A, hold, meter sand into B, allow B to provide useful heat, then reverse the assembly to return the sand. Mechanically, the combined assembly still needs a stable cradle and positive locking; it is not suitable for manual inversion while hot.

Bottom line: adding the third shell, an external industrial mineral-wool jacket and 40 kg of sand substantially improves the energy budget. It can plausibly support 300 W over the intended ten-hour transfer. Achieving 300 W for eighteen hours after an eight-hour hold is much less certain: 200–250 mm of insulation gets the calculation closer, but the remaining energy margin is small. The next meaningful test is to measure the real eight-hour standby loss and the receiver's heat output, rather than adding more steel shells on the assumption that each one automatically provides a large insulation improvement.
