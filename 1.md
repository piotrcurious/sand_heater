The biggest improvement is to separate two problems that are currently coupled: heating the sand rapidly to 800°C, and extracting heat slowly enough to maintain at least 300 W for 18 hours. A very hot heating element solves only the first problem.

My preferred inexpensive architecture would use a proper high-temperature FeCrAl heating element, a short and wide sand chamber, distributed heat collection, and an engineered thermal break to the outer enclosure. Tungsten has an impressive maximum operating temperature, but it is not automatically a better thermal-storage heater.

Why the tungsten halogen lamp is not the best solution

Tungsten can run at a very high filament temperature, but a 500 W lamp still delivers only 500 W of electrical power. In an enclosed thermal store, almost all that energy eventually becomes heat; the important questions are how quickly it transfers to the sand and whether the parts survive the operating conditions.

Adding a second glass tube changes the lamp's heat-transfer conditions and can cause overheating. A probe does not turn a lighting component into a furnace element. A purpose-built ceramic-supported resistance element is the more controllable and repairable solution.

One important distinction: the element can run hotter than the sand without needing to be made of tungsten. FeCrAl is specifically designed for high-temperature electrical heating, and its published operating range is already sufficient for this application.

The element layout matters at least as much as the alloy. A single 500 W source may create a very hot region near the heater while leaving the rest of the charge comparatively cold. Several distributed ceramic-supported elements are a better match for rapid, uniform charging.

### 4\. Passive output regulation could reduce the mass and power requirement

The fixed end surface has an unavoidable drawback: it delivers much more heat when the store is hot than when it is nearly depleted. That is why I would also consider a passive, temperature-responsive shutter over part of the radiating face.

The shutter would expose less of the surface at high temperatures and progressively uncover more area as the store cools. This is not a simple unregulated radiator, but it could remain fully passive if actuated by a suitable temperature-responsive mechanical device.

With output regulated close to 300 W, the useful target becomes 5.4 kWh rather than the considerably larger total energy released by a fixed radiator that starts near 1 kW.

For that alternative, 45–50 kg of sand, roughly 29–32 litres, and around 3 kW of charging power is a plausible starting point. It has less margin for losses, so actual measurements would be essential. The trade-off is more mechanical complexity in exchange for a smaller storage mass and lower electrical input.

### 5\. Argon and the stainless-steel flange

Your choice of stainless steel for the flange is directionally sensible because it conducts heat much less readily than copper or aluminium. However, low thermal conductivity alone does not make a broad flange thermally negligible.

### Argon is useful, but it does not replace vacuum insulation

Argon has a lower thermal conductivity than air: NIST data gives approximately 17.7 mW/(m·K) for argon versus 26.4 mW/(m·K) for air at 300 K. At 600 K, the corresponding figures are approximately 30.3 and 46.0 mW/(m·K). That is a meaningful reduction, but not an order-of-magnitude improvement.

Filling the annulus with argon would also eliminate much of the benefit of whatever partial vacuum you had achieved. If the chamber is sealed with gas at room temperature and that gas subsequently heats substantially, the pressure can rise considerably; in the idealized case of the entire gas volume reaching 800°C, the pressure would rise by a factor of about 3.66 relative to its value at 20°C.

I would prioritise, in this order, reducing the flange's thermal conductance, limiting radiation across the annulus with suitable high-temperature radiation shields, and using an appropriate high-temperature insulation strategy. Argon makes most sense as a controlled inert atmosphere, or as part of a deliberately engineered low-pressure system—not as a substitute for insulation.

### 6\. Charging power and practical construction

Heating 80 kg of sand to 800°C requires about 17.2 kWh, before heating the steel container or accounting for losses. A 10–15 kg steel liner could add roughly another 1.3–2 kWh, depending on its average heat capacity and final temperature.

That means the idealised input requirement is already near 19 kWh. Over five hours, that is approximately 3.8 kW before charging losses. In practice I would investigate a 4–5 kW heating system, with proper high-temperature elements, independent over-temperature protection and instrumentation.

At 230 V, 4 kW draws approximately 17.4 A, while 5 kW draws about 21.7 A. This is beyond the rating of an ordinary 16 A circuit. The supply arrangement must be designed for the actual load, or the charging power and schedule must be changed.

I would also avoid sealing a hot sand charge containing moisture or an uncontrolled gas volume. At 800°C, the inner vessel, its welds and its heater feedthroughs need to be suitable for sustained high-temperature cycling. The outer evacuated shell also needs an explicit design against external-pressure collapse.

For the first prototype, use thermocouples at the heater, centre of the sand, rear of the charge, radiating end and outer shell. They will reveal whether the limiting factor is heating power, heat diffusion, the flange, or the extraction path.

My recommendation: start with the 70–80 kg, shallow cylindrical design if maintaining at least 300 W for 18 hours with a simple passive end is the priority. Use FeCrAl on ceramic supports, distribute the heat through the charge, and treat the flange as a major thermal-design problem rather than assuming stainless steel alone will solve it.
